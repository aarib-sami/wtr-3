Decision Trees

A decision tree is a bunch of yes/no questions, plus the math that decides which question to ask next.

Aarib Sami, Bachelor of Computing, University of Guelph

Introduction

Why I'm writing about this

This was my third work term, summer 2026. Amdocs wasn't comfortable with me writing about the work I did, so this report is about decision trees instead. I used it to actually learn about them and how they work.

I'm going to walk through how a tree gets built, how it decides which question to ask, why a tree that fits the training data perfectly is usually a bad tree, and when I'd actually reach for one. The example I'll keep coming back to is Figure 1: should I bike to campus or take the bus?

In summary, my findings were that a tree asks yes/no questions. Each question is chosen because it separates the labels a bit better than the last one. If you let it keep asking forever it starts memorizing random details. If you cut it back, you get a small set of rules you can actually read.

The topic

Why decision trees

I've used other models before. I did KNN for a course project (the zoo animal dataset), and I trained a small neural net on my own for FireGuard. They're fine at predicting. The annoying part is that if someone asks why the model said what it said, I don't really have an answer. KNN is "these nearby rows were similar." The net is a pile of weights. With a tree you can just read the path: it's raining, so bus. That's the part I wanted to understand this term.

Supervised learning in general is: you have labelled examples, and you want to label new ones. A tree does that by repeatedly splitting the data. Numeric splits look like "temperature >= 12". Categorical ones look like "raining? yes/no". Follow the path down to a leaf and that's the prediction. Figure 1 is basically how someone may decide to get to campus, written as a model.

The two names you'll see a lot are ID3 / C4.5 (Quinlan), which score splits with information gain, and CART (Breiman et al.), which is what most libraries actually implement. CART uses Gini, only does binary splits, and handles both classification and regression. sklearn's DecisionTreeClassifier is CART.

People usually jump straight to random forests or gradient boosting, which are just a lot of trees combined. Those are stronger on accuracy. I wanted to get one tree down first, because if I don't understand the single tree, the ensemble is just a black box with extra steps.

The model

How a tree works

Start with all your training rows in one group. That's the root. Then the algorithm asks a question about one feature, sends the "yes" rows one way and the "no" rows the other, and repeats on each group. It's greedy: at every node it picks the best split for that node only. It does not look ahead to see if a slightly worse first question would make the rest of the tree nicer. That's a tradeoff. It's fast, and it's also why two similar datasets can grow totally different trees.

It stops when you tell it to. Usual knobs are max depth, min samples in a leaf, or "this split barely helps." The groups at the bottom are leaves. For classification the leaf takes the majority class (or you can keep the class probabilities). For regression it takes the average of the target in that leaf.

For a numeric feature it doesn't try every real number. It sorts the unique values and tries cutoffs in between them. That's what the slider in Figure 3 is doing, just by hand. Categorical features are a partition of the values. Missing values are their own mess: some implementations add a third branch, some use a backup split, some make you impute first. I didn't go deep on missing data for this report. The toy mornings are all filled in.

None of the structure is that new if you've done data structures. It's recursion. The part I didn't really have practice with is checking whether those rules still work on rows the tree hasn't seen, instead of assuming a deeper tree is a better tree.

Scoring a split

At each node it tries a bunch of possible questions: every feature, and for numeric features those midpoints between sorted values. For each option it checks how mixed the two sides would be. "Mixed" means the labels in that group are still a coin flip. A pure node is all bike or all bus. The best question is the one that makes the children the least mixed, weighted by how many rows went each way.

Two common ways to measure that. Entropy is H = − Σ pk log2 pk, where the pk are the class proportions in that node. A 50/50 node is 1 bit. A pure node is 0. Information gain is parent entropy minus the weighted average of the children. Gini is 1 − Σ pk2, which is the chance you'd mislabel a row if you drew a label at random from that node. CART uses Gini. ID3 uses entropy. In practice they usually pick the same first split. I used Gini in the slider below because I didn't want to deal with logs while dragging a threshold around.

Here's ten weekday mornings, bike vs bus. Rain should be the first question. Temperature is in there too so you can see why it loses at the root even though it still matters later.

Before any split it's 5 bike and 5 bus, so entropy is 1 and Gini is 0.5. If I split on rain, the rainy side is all bus (mornings 7 to 10, entropy 0). The dry side is 5 bike and 1 bus (morning 6 is the cold dry bus), entropy 0.650. Information gain is 1 − 0.6 × 0.650 = 0.610. Weighted Gini after that split is 0.167.

Same table, but split on temperature at 12 C instead. The cold side is mornings 6, 8, and 9, all bus, so entropy 0. The warm side is 5 bike and 2 bus (those two are the rainy 19 C and 22 C mornings). That's 7 rows, entropy about 0.863. Information gain is only 0.396. So rain wins at the root, and temperature becomes the next question on the dry side, which is exactly Figure 1.

Try a split

Forget rain for a second. Same idea, but only temperature, 14 mornings instead of 10, so the plot isn't empty. Each dot is a morning. Drag the line. The number that matters is weighted Gini: how mixed the left side is, how mixed the right side is, averaged by size. Lower is cleaner.

The algorithm would try the midpoints between the sorted temperatures and keep the cutoff with the lowest weighted Gini. On this set that's 12 C (the dashed line). There's one hot day on the right where I still took the bus, so that side isn't perfectly pure. Gini there is 0.198 instead of 0. That's why the tree would ask another question after this one, which is the whole recursive part. If you park the slider around 18 C you'll see it's a worse cut than 12, even though it looks more "in the middle."

Growth and pruning

If you never stop splitting, the tree will eventually make a rule out of noise. Morning 6 in the table is dry and 8 degrees and I took the bus. Maybe I just slept in. That shouldn't become a permanent rule for next February.

Two ways to deal with that. You can stop it early while it grows, using max depth, min samples in a leaf, or a minimum impurity drop. That's simple, but you can stop too early and miss a split that only pays off after the next one. The other option is to grow a big tree first and then cut branches off.

CART's version is cost complexity pruning. You score a tree as training error plus α times the number of leaves. Turn α up and extra leaves start costing more than they're worth, so they get removed. You pick α on a validation set, not on the training set, otherwise you're just overfitting again. The left tree below still has a leaf for one row. The right one is Figure 1: two questions, that's it.

sklearn will do this for you with ccp_alpha. I read how it works. I did not reimplement the pruning loop, which is still the hole in goal 2.

Using them

When I would use one

I'd use a single tree when I care about being able to explain the answer. If someone is going to ask "why did it say that," a path of questions is a lot easier than a neural net. That's also why trees show up as a first model, or as a way to explain a stronger model after the fact.

If I only care about accuracy and I have a lot of features, I probably wouldn't stop at one tree. A random forest averages a bunch of trees trained on random subsets of rows and features, so the instability of one tree mostly washes out. Gradient boosting builds trees in sequence to fix the last one's mistakes and is usually the accuracy default. Sometimes even a linear model is enough, and it's simpler to deploy.

They have real limitations, and I didn't want to skip those. Every split is a straight cut on one feature, so if the real boundary is diagonal you get an ugly staircase. They're unstable: change a few training rows and the first split can change. If a feature has a ton of categories, the tree can latch onto it just because it's easy to look pure (that's why C4.5 uses gain ratio instead of raw information gain). And a deep tree on small data will memorize, which is the whole pruning section.

I still think they're useful. For this commute example, two questions is the whole model. That's the kind of thing I'd actually want to ship if the point is the rules, not the last percentage point of accuracy.

Conclusions

What I got out of this

I couldn't write about the job, so I used the report to get decision trees straight. Going in, I could tell you they "split on features." Coming out, I can score a split on a table, tell you why rain beats temperature at the root, and tell you why that leaf built on a single row in Figure 4 shouldn't survive.

What I want to remember going into next term is this. A tree is greedy questions plus a rule for when to stop. If it never stops, it memorizes. Pruning is how you get back something you can read. Forests and boosting are for when you care more about the score than the path. I can do the scoring by hand now. I still haven't implemented pruning myself, and that's what I want to come back to.
